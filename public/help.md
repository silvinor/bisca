# How to play Bisca

## The Game

> The rules below are the ones the author learned in his region. Bisca is played a little differently from region to region, and this version of the app does not try to cover every local variation.

### Players & deck

The game is played by 2, 3, or 4 players depending on [game mode](#game_mode). It is played with a common 52-card deck (but with the 8's, 9's, and 10's of each suit removed, creating the 40 cards required to play) or a Spanish or Italian 40-card deck. The main objective of the game is to accumulate more points than the opponent, based on the cards that are captured and forfeited. It uses the Ace with value of 11 points and the seven (called the "bisca", "manilha" or "seta") with a value of 10 points, the King worth 4 points, Jack worth 3 points, and the Queen worth 2 points [^1].

### The deal

The game is played anticlockwise.

The first dealer is chosen by draw of the high Joker [^2], and the turn to deal passes to the right after each hand. The dealer shuffles the cards and the player at the dealer's left cuts. The dealer then gives 3 cards (or more depending on [game mode](#game_mode)) to each player, one card at a time, beginning with the player to dealer's right, going around anticlockwise and ending with the dealer. The next card is then turned face up, and its suit becomes trumps (called *trunfo*). It is then placed at the bottom of the deck, partially visible.

In the Sueca game mode the card top of the split deck becomes the trump card, and is placed face up on the dealers side - the dealer keeps the remaining 9 cards to themselves and may plat the trump card at any time.

### The play

The player who cut the deck leads by playing one of the cards they were dealt. Each other player then plays one card in turn, anticlockwise. Once everyone has played, the winner of the *trick* [^3] is decided as follows:

  1. If any trumps were played, the player who played the highest trump wins.
  2. If the cards are all the same suit, the highest-ranked card wins (A 7 K J Q 6 5 4 3 2).
  3. If the cards are of different suits and no trumps were played, the highest card in the same suit as the first card played wins. Cards of any other suit cannot win.
  4. Players do **not** need to play a card of the same suit as the first card played (unlike games such as Bridge). The exception is Sueca, where players must follow suit if they can [^4]. If they have no card of that suit, they may play any card.

Captured cards are placed face down near the player who won them for later score counting - these cards are not played again in that deal.

After each trick (except in Sueca), each player takes a new card from the top of the deck (first the player who won the cards from the table, then anticlockwise) and this is repeated until the cards in the deck (including the final trump card) are taken up. Once the deck is empty, the remaining cards are played out without drawing.

The player who won the trick then leads the next trick.

At the end of the game, each player (or team) adds up the points on the cards they captured. The player (or team) with more points wins; the total is always 120.

## Difficulty

This setting controls how well the computer opponents play. The computer never sees the cards in your hand.

| Difficulty | How the computer plays |
| --- | --- |
| Easy | The computer mostly plays at random and does not plan ahead. It does not play the way a real player would. |
| Normal | The computer holds on to high-value cards and plays them at the right moment. |
| Hard | The computer plays as in Normal, but also remembers which cards have been played and looks for the best moves towards the end of the game. |

{.table}

## Match Count {#match_count}

This setting controls how long a match lasts. A *game* is one deal: the deck is shuffled and dealt, and all the cards are played out. A *set* is won by the first player (or team) to win 4 games.

| Match Count | How it is played |
| --- | --- |
| Single Game | One game is played, from a single shuffle of the deck. |
| One Set | Games are played until one player (or team) has won 4 games, which wins the set. |
| Best of Three | Three sets are played, even if one side already leads 2–0. The side that wins more sets wins the match. |
| First to Four | Up to 7 sets are played. The first side to win 4 sets wins the match. |

{.table}

### Scoring

The winner of each game scores one game point. Some modes score two game points for a large win instead: 82 or more card points in Nine's Bisca, and 91 or more in Sueca. If a game ends 60–60, nobody scores, and the game is replayed. The first to reach 4 game points wins the set, and the next set starts again from zero.

### Score keeping

The score is kept using one of two methods:

- **Crosses and dots** ("Cruzes e pontos"): two crosses are drawn, marked *Us* ("Nós") and *Them* ("Eles"). Each game won is marked with a dot on one of the outer points of the cross. The first side to fill all four points wins the set.
- **Comb and dots** ("Pente e pontos"): a "comb" is drawn as a horizontal line crossed by four vertical lines, one for each game of the set. Each game won is marked with a dot on a vertical line: above the horizontal line for your own wins, and below it for your opponent's. Combs are also often drawn with 10 lines, for a match played to 10 games.

## History

Bisca (a Portuguese version of the Italian game Bríscola) is a card game based on the Italian (40 card) deck.

Bisca is a Portuguese point-trick game, closely related to the Italian Briscola and the Spanish Brisca. All three are played with a 40-card pack, but in Bisca the 10-point card ranking between the Ace and the King is the Seven rather than the Three. It is also closely related to Sueca, which can be thought of as 10-card Bisca, and to some African games influenced by Portuguese contact, such as Albastini.

The family most likely descends from Brusquembille, a French game whose rules were published in 1718. Brusquembille evolved into a game called Brisque, which in turn gave rise to Brisca, Bezique and the more elaborate Briscan; Briscola is described as a little-changed descendant of Brusquembille. Another theory proposes an Italian origin, from the game of Bazzica, which is similar to Bezique.

Bisca was originally played with Portuguese-suited cards. Portugal began making its own playing cards at the Real Fábrica de Cartas de Jogar, founded in Lisbon in 1769. From around 1800, Portuguese-suited packs were gradually replaced by the French deck, and popular games such as Bisca were adapted to the French deck. Portuguese cards also travelled overseas to Brazil, Java and Japan, where the Japanese word for playing cards, *karuta*, comes from the Portuguese *carta*.

Four-player Bisca was played in Portugal in the 20th century, and a four-player variant is still played in the state of Espírito Santo in Brazil. Brisca and its cousins remain widespread across the Mediterranean and Latin America.

[^1]: When the Italian and Spanish cards are mapped onto the French deck, the Jack outranks the Queen. The Jack's picture most resembles the Knight, while the Queen is matched to the Knave, which in the old Portuguese decks (the *sotas*) was always shown as a woman. This can confuse players who are used to the Queen outranking the Jack.

[^2]: Most packs include two Jokers, a practice that became standard in the late 1940s for the game of Canasta. The two are often printed differently: one in full colour and one in a single colour, or one red and one black. Where the Jokers need to be ranked, the full-colour or red Joker is usually the "high" Joker and outranks the other. Some packs print both Jokers the same, and German and Austrian packs have included three since the 1950s. In this app's decks, the high Joker is the more detailed of the two, though this varies from deck to deck.

[^3]: A *trick* is one round of play in which each player plays one card.

[^4]: Breaking this rule is called a *renúncia* (a revoke). Its penalties don't apply in this app, because the app won't let you play an invalid card.

[^5]: A random 2 is removed and shown at the beginning of the match, so it may turn out to be of the trump suit of subsequent games, leaving 9 trumps instead of 10. Since no one knows which suit will be trumps, this does not favour any player.
